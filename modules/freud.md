# Freud: Dream Theory and Method

## Historical frame

Freud's *The Interpretation of Dreams* (first published 1900) developed a psychoanalytic theory and method. It is historically influential, not a settled scientific account. Say “Freud proposed…” rather than presenting it as established explanation.

## Main concepts in Freud's framework

- **Manifest content:** the dream as remembered and reported.
- **Latent dream-thoughts/content:** associations and thoughts Freud's method sought to uncover behind the report. Do not assume there is one hidden message.
- **Free association:** ask what comes to mind for each element, including trivial, awkward, or contradictory thoughts. The dreamer's associations lead; do not impose a ready-made meaning.
- **Day residues:** Freud emphasized recent experiences as material that may be woven into dreams, alongside older concerns and memories.
- **Dream-work:** Freud's proposed transformation of dream-thoughts into remembered content. Concepts include condensation (one element combining several associations), displacement (emphasis/emotion shifting), representability (abstract thoughts rendered as images), and secondary revision (the remembered story made more coherent).
- **Wish-fulfilment and censorship:** central Freudian hypotheses, especially in *The Interpretation of Dreams*. They are not universal scientific laws; nightmares and other counterexamples require nuance even within psychoanalytic discussion.
- **Symbolism:** Freud discussed recurring motifs, but do not reduce his method to a fixed “X means Y” dictionary. Freud Museum London describes his method as following the dreamer's associations.

## Safe use

Offer a Freudian question, not a verdict: “If you follow Freud's free-association method, what comes to mind first when you think of that image?” If invited, discuss possible condensation or displacement as hypotheses. Never assert repressed sexual wishes, childhood trauma, or family conflict from an image alone. Identify Freud's developmental theories as historical theoretical commitments, not facts about the individual.
