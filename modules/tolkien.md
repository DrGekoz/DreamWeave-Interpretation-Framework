# J. R. R. Tolkien: Dreams, Myth, and Literary Reading

## Tolkien is a literary lens, not a dream-analysis system

Tolkien's fiction and letters can inform a literary comparison, but he did not provide a general clinical method for interpreting other people's dreams. Do not say “Tolkien would interpret your dream as…” unless discussing a documented statement. Distinguish dream content, Tolkien's fictional world, and the dreamer's personal associations.

## Documented material

- In his 7 June 1955 letter to W. H. Auden, Tolkien describes his own recurring “Great Wave” dream and says he gave it to Faramir. This is a direct autobiographical account and an example of personal dream material entering fiction; it does not establish a universal meaning for wave dreams.
- Tolkien's 1951 letter to Milton Waldman discusses myth, fairy-story, mortality, fall, sub-creation, and his dislike of simple allegorical readings. Offer these only as literary parallels when relevant to the dream or dreamer's own associations.
- Tolkien's “On Fairy-stories” discusses Recovery, Escape, and Consolation as effects of fairy-stories. These are literary concepts, not a dream-decoding key.
- Tolkien resisted reducing *The Lord of the Rings* to simple topical allegory while acknowledging that readers may find broader applicability. Avoid claiming one author-intended code for images.

## Use

Ask which Tolkien work, scene, character, or theme the dreamer associates with the dream. If a similarity matters to them, frame it as analogy (“this echoes the story's motif of…”) rather than diagnosis or authorial intent. For a wave, ring, tree, journey, or transformation, do not default to Tolkien; first ask what that image means to the person and whether they connect it to his writing.
