DreamWeave is a research-aware dream interpretation skill created by **Joseph Williams (DrGekoz)**. It brings distinct interpretive traditions into one careful reading without pretending that neuroscience, psychoanalysis, a sacred text, a folktale, and a modern esoteric book are the same kind of evidence.

The aim is not to announce what a dream “really means.” It is to help the dreamer explore plausible readings, understand where each reading comes from, and distinguish personally meaningful ideas from claims that have independent evidence.

> Core rule: **Convergence raises salience, not proof.** A motif recurring across traditions can be worth exploring. Its recurrence alone does not make a supernatural, psychological, or predictive explanation true.

## Why DreamWeave

- **Evidence is labeled.** Every interpretation is identified as empirical research, psychoanalytic theory, religious/philosophical belief, myth/folklore, literary analogy, modern esotericism, pseudohistorical/pseudoscientific claim, or personal speculation.
- **Frameworks stay distinct.** Each tradition gets its own reading before any synthesis; disagreements are not erased.
- **The dreamer leads.** Personal associations, emotion, and waking context matter more than a generic symbol dictionary.
- **Sources and provenance matter.** Ancient texts, later commentaries, living faith, modern occult works, and fictional world-building are not conflated.
- **The synthesis is transparent.** Shared threads and differences are shown, then a clearly tentative “Joseph’s Interpretation” can combine them when requested.

## Method

DreamWeave uses a seven-stage reasoning process. It is a structured interpretive method, not a scientifically validated dream-decoding algorithm.

### 1. Preserve the actual report

Restate only what the dreamer reported. Keep unknowns visible: mood, setting, sensory details, recent experiences, and the dreamer's own associations. Never fill gaps with invented backstory.

### 2. Start with the dreamer’s associations

Ask what the images mean to this person, what they felt during and after the dream, and what has been happening in waking life. A turnip might be food, a disliked vegetable, something grown in a garden, a joke, or nothing personally salient. Do not assume a universal meaning.

### 3. Establish the evidence floor

Offer the scientific lens first. Research can study when and how dreams are reported, their relationships with waking concerns, emotion, memory, sleep, and nightmare treatments. It cannot currently certify that a particular dream symbol has one fixed meaning or prove that a given dream predicts an event.

### 4. Read each requested framework on its own terms

For each lens, identify the source, period, tradition, and type of claim. Summarize what that tradition actually says before applying it. Connect only the dream details for which a relevant source or clearly marked analogy exists. State when a framework offers no specific interpretation.

### 5. Label the claim, not just the section

A section heading alone is insufficient: distinguish an empirical result from a theory, a tradition's belief, a documented narrative motif, and the analyst's own conjecture. Label uncertain applications as tentative.

### 6. Compare after separate readings

List common motifs only after completing the individual readings. Check whether similarity could come from cultural borrowing, shared source texts, broad language, selective attention, translation, or common human experience. Then list the meaningful disagreements and unresolved alternatives.

### 7. Synthesize without upgrading certainty

“Joseph’s Interpretation” is a transparent personal synthesis, not an oracle or factual verdict. It may combine the dreamer's context, cautious psychological hypotheses, empirical observations, and clearly named mythic/religious resonances. A theme repeated across systems does not become scientifically established through repetition.

## Evidence labels

| Label | What it means | What it does not mean |
|---|---|---|
| **Empirical finding** | An observation or result from a specified research method and population | That science has decoded this individual dream |
| **Supported association** | A pattern with empirical support, with scope and limits stated | A universal symbol rule or causal certainty |
| **Emerging hypothesis** | A plausible research account still under testing | A settled fact |
| **Psychoanalytic theory** | A named historical or contemporary interpretive model, such as Freud or Jung | A proven mechanism or diagnosis |
| **Religious/philosophical belief** | A view attributed to a named text, lineage, school, or community | A claim independently verified by science, or a claim shared by all adherents |
| **Myth/folklore** | A motif in a documented narrative or tradition | A prediction about the dreamer |
| **Literary analogy** | A comparison with a fictional work or author's world-building | An author's clinical or religious doctrine |
| **Modern esoteric claim** | A modern occult interpretation, clearly attributed and dated where possible | Ancient provenance or scientific validation |
| **Pseudohistorical / pseudoscientific claim** | An unsupported claim presented as established history or science | A label to dismiss religion, myth, or symbolic meaning |
| **Personal speculation** | A tentative possibility offered for reflection | A hidden truth the dreamer must accept |

## Frameworks

The skill covers the following **15 broad framework and method categories**. Several categories contain named subtraditions and source-specific lenses; these are not all interchangeable or exhaustive representations of world traditions.

1. **Dream science and clinical sleep research** — dream reports, waking-life continuity, emotion/memory hypotheses, methodological limits, and evidence-based nightmare-care references. Science is a framework for evaluating evidence, not a symbolic dictionary.
2. **Freud and psychoanalysis** — wish-fulfilment theory, manifest/latent content, condensation, displacement, secondary revision, day residues, and free association, presented as historically influential theory rather than settled neuroscience.
3. **Jung and analytical psychology** — personal associations, compensation, archetypal amplification as Jung's theory, and the historical role of *The Red Book* as a record of Jung's inner work, not a universal codebook.
4. **Daoist / Taoist philosophy** — especially the *Zhuangzi*'s butterfly dream as a philosophical problem of perspective, transformation, and knowing; not a standardized Taoist dream dictionary.
5. **Buddhist traditions** — selected Buddhist textual and contemplative perspectives, including Tibetan dream yoga where relevant; distinguish practice, doctrine, and modern psychological analogy.
6. **Chinese folklore and divination** — historical dream manuals and culturally situated omen traditions, including Zhougong-related material; distinguish historical practice from empirical prediction.
7. **Hindu traditions** — selected Upanishadic/philosophical discussions of waking, dreaming, and consciousness; do not treat Hindu traditions as one school.
8. **Jewish traditions** — Biblical material and selected rabbinic discussions of dreams, with attention to interpretive plurality and historical context.
9. **Christian traditions** — Biblical dream narratives and selected Christian interpretive traditions; distinguish scriptural narrative, denominational theology, later commentary, and personal faith.
10. **Islamic traditions** — selected textual and interpretive traditions, carefully sourced and distinguished from modern popular symbol lists.
11. **Norse / Viking-era literary traditions** — dreams and foreknowledge as represented in particular Old Norse sagas; literary/historical evidence, not a uniform Viking manual.
12. **Ancient Egyptian and ancient Near Eastern traditions** — Egyptian dream papyri, Mesopotamian omen and dream texts, and source-specific historical interpretation.
13. **Greek and Roman traditions** — including Artemidorus and ancient medical, philosophical, and divinatory approaches, treated as historical systems.
14. **Selected African and Indigenous traditions** — carefully attributed, bounded examples from specific sources and communities, including Amerindian, Aboriginal Australian, and Māori scholarship. No pan-African, pan-Indigenous, or pan-Aboriginal “dream code” is asserted; community knowledge and protocols matter.
15. **Literary and modern esoteric lenses** — Tolkien as literary world-building, not a dream-analysis method; plus explicitly labeled modern occult material such as Doreal's *Emerald Tablets of Thoth the Atlantean*.

### Important source distinctions

- **Thoth and Egypt:** Ancient Egyptian dream manuals are distinct from later Hermetic literature. The medieval *Emerald Tablet* has an Arabic/Latin textual history; it is not a verified pharaonic artifact or a dream manual. M. Doreal's *Emerald Tablets of Thoth the Atlantean* has a modern publication record and no verified ancient tablet provenance. It can be read as modern esotericism, not established ancient Egyptian history.
- **1 Enoch:** Its Dream Visions (including the Animal Apocalypse) belong to ancient Jewish apocalyptic literature and a specific allegorical-historical setting. They are not a universal symbol guide or evidence that a present-day dream predicts the apocalypse. Canonical status varies; 1 Enoch is canonical in the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo tradition.
- **Anunna / Anunnaki:** Ancient Sumerian and Akkadian texts describe a group of deities whose roles vary by source and period. Modern alien-visitor or “ancient astronaut” claims are not the same tradition and are not supported as established history by mainstream archaeology or Assyriology.
- **Tolkien:** His legendarium can provide a literary comparison when requested. It is fiction/world-building, not a clinical framework, revelation, or evidence about the dreamer's future.

## Interpretation output

For a requested full analysis, DreamWeave follows this order:

1. **Disclaimer and method** — exploratory, not diagnosis, prophecy, or universal decoding.
2. **Dream as reported** — accurate summary plus explicitly named unknowns.
3. **Scientific lens** — relevant evidence, confidence, limitations, and what cannot be inferred.
4. **Separate framework readings** — each requested/relevant lens with its own claim labels, source context, tentative application, and limitations.
5. **Cross-framework common threads** — only real overlaps, with the supporting lenses and a caveat about independence.
6. **Differences Summary** — divergent explanations, types of authority/evidence, and unresolved plausible meanings.
7. **Joseph’s Interpretation** — optional, clearly personal amalgamation; distinguish strongest observation, useful psychological hypothesis, cultural resonance, and unknowns.
8. **Open reflection questions** — only when useful and welcome.

A short casual request gets a concise answer. A full comparative report uses the complete format; no irrelevant tradition is added just to increase the framework count.

## Example of the reasoning standard

For “Joe was a farmer and turned turnips into a baby,” the skill must not claim that a turnip universally symbolizes fertility, that a baby proves pregnancy, or that cross-cultural agreement validates a supernatural explanation. It can say, as a **personal possibility**, that farming and transformation might evoke nurturing or change if those associations fit Cindy's feelings and life. The scientific lens can discuss dream construction and waking concerns without claiming to decode the turnip. Historical or religious traditions may be added only where a source genuinely supports the comparison, and otherwise the skill should say “no specific source-based reading found.”

## Guardrails

DreamWeave never diagnoses, infers repressed memories, asserts trauma from a dream, predicts pregnancy/death/infidelity, claims knowledge of another person's intentions, or states as fact that a dream came from God, ancestors, spirits, or the dead. When recurring nightmares, trauma symptoms, or sleep disruption are distressing, it recommends seeking qualified clinical support; it does not prescribe treatment. Indigenous and living religious traditions are described respectfully and specifically, never flattened into aesthetic “exotic” symbolism.

## Install

Copy `SKILL.md` and its `modules/` directory into a Hermes skill folder, for example:

```text
<hermes skills directory>/creative/dreamweave-interpretation/
```

Keep the relative `modules/` directory beside `SKILL.md`. Restart/reload Hermes if the skill registry does not refresh automatically, then verify it is available with the skill listing/view commands documented for your Hermes version.

## Research and source policy

This is a selective, source-led synthesis, **not an expert-reviewed systematic review**. Sources include empirical studies/reviews, academic books and chapters, institutional catalogs, primary texts, and community-specific scholarship. Their evidentiary roles differ. Historical texts document what a tradition said or practiced; they do not by themselves validate metaphysical claims. The skill cites sources in `SKILL.md` and topic modules. Recheck links, editions, translations, and current clinical guidance before relying on them.
